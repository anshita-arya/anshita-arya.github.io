The four phases are Together's; the split shown between them is proportional, since only the totals are published. Queueing is reported as excess concurrency rather than a predicted TTFT, because turning concurrency into a latency number needs the engine's own batching behaviour, which is not public. Saturation is treated as a single number, and it is really a curve: a replica is bounded hard by KV cache memory (weights first, then concurrent sequences divide what is left, so long contexts fit fewer), and softly by the compute knee where batching stops being free. In practice you get the number from a load test, by pushing concurrency until p95 leaves your SLO — which makes it part physics and part policy. The distance between your target and that limit is the headroom the autoscaler spends while new replicas start. It also treats every request as costing the same, which is the weak point of in-flight concurrency as a signal: prompt length drives prefill and output length drives decode, so eight short chat turns and eight long-context agent calls are the same number and nowhere near the same load. Where per-request cost varies that much, a token-weighted signal or TTFT describes pressure better than a request count does.

You can't autoscale your way out of a cold start. So what do you do instead?

Together's autoscaling post makes an argument most platform docs avoid. It says, plainly, that the usual advice does not work here.

"Cold starts can take several minutes. A new replica has to be placed on a GPU node, pull tens of gigabytes of weights, load them into VRAM, and warm up. This means that you can't scale your way out of a spike, because by the time it arrives it's already too late to scale up."

Then it gives you a framework: scale on signals the inference engine understands rather than GPU utilization, go up eagerly and come down patiently, and if your spike outruns your cold start, treat it as a capacity-shape question "better answered before launch day."

That last line is the one I kept rereading. It is correct, and it stops exactly where the reader's problem starts. Nobody knows their capacity shape. They know their peak. The simulator above is the missing arithmetic; the rest of this is how to use it.

Step 1: measure your slope, not your peak

Every team can tell you their peak. Almost nobody can tell you how fast they get there, which is the number that actually decides whether autoscaling is a viable strategy for them.

The quantity you need is the rate of change of concurrency — how many additional in-flight requests arrive per second during your worst ramp. Peak tells you how much capacity you need eventually. Slope tells you whether you can acquire it in time.

If you have request logs, this is a short query: bucket by second, compute in-flight, take the steepest sustained rise over a window the length of your cold start. If you don't have logs yet, you can usually reason about it from the shape of the trigger. A marketing email lands and the ramp is minutes. A coding agent kicks off and fans one user action into fifty model calls, and the ramp is seconds.

Step 2: add up your reaction budget

Together breaks a cold start into four phases: GPU placement, weight download, engine load, warmup. The platform's event feed timestamps each one. The measured numbers they publish for a single H100 on a warm cluster are a useful anchor: a 9B catalog model reaches READY in about 86 seconds, a fresh 18GB fine-tune in about 145, and it takes another 26 to 40 seconds before the first token comes back through routing. Larger models take proportionally longer, up to roughly 7 to 14 minutes for frontier MoE models.

Your reaction budget is that cold start plus your scale_up_window, because the autoscaler waits for pressure to persist before it acts. Call it detection plus startup. Now compare it to your ramp:

  • Ramp longer than the budget. Autoscaling works. Tune the metric and the windows as the post describes, and you are done.
  • Ramp shorter than the budget. Autoscaling is structurally unable to help you, and no amount of tuning changes that. You are not choosing a configuration. You are choosing who absorbs the cost.

Step 3: pick which cost you are paying

Once you are in the second case, there are only four levers, and all of them cost something. The useful move is to be explicit about which currency you are paying in.

Hold headroom. Set min_replicas high enough to absorb the spike with capacity that is already warm. You pay for idle GPU, continuously, for an event that happens occasionally. This is the honest default and usually the right one for anything with a p95 SLO.

Lower the target. Run every replica slacker so there is spare concurrency in the system at all times. The same bill as headroom, arriving under a different name, with the advantage that the slack is distributed rather than sitting in one spare replica.

Shrink the budget. Attack the cold start itself. A smaller model, quantized weights, pinned and cached weights, a warm pool. Together's platform does the fleet-side version of this already: they rebuilt the model caching and distribution layer so weights are shared across the fleet and proactively warmed before a deployment needs them, which they report as roughly 4x faster warm starts.

Shed load deliberately. The lever people reach for last and should consider first. If you cannot serve the spike at full quality, decide in advance what degrades: queue with a published wait, fall back to a smaller model, or send the overflow somewhere that is already warm.

The pattern I would want

That last lever is where I think there is something missing from the current story, and it comes from how this problem was solved on CPU.

Having built on serverless CPU platforms, the standard answer to cold start there is a small number of pre-warmed instances that absorb the first burst while the elastic tier catches up. The architecture pairs a reserved floor with an elastic ceiling, and the floor exists purely to cover the reaction gap.

The inference equivalent is sitting right there in the product line. A dedicated endpoint gives you predictable latency and a cold start measured in minutes. Serverless gives you a shared warm pool with no cold start at all, at the cost of latency variance under load. Those are usually presented as a choice. For spiky traffic they look more like two halves of one design: dedicated for the baseline, with overflow spilling to serverless during the window where your replicas are still starting.

You would need the model available on both tiers, you would accept higher and more variable TTFT for the overflow portion, and you would need routing that can make that decision quickly enough to matter. I do not know whether that is supported today or whether the routing economics work. But the shape of it is familiar from the CPU era, and the gap it covers is precisely the one the autoscaling post identifies and then, reasonably, leaves to the reader.

What I would add to the original post

  1. A worked example with the arithmetic shown. The post gives the 10x-in-90-seconds case as an illustration. Running it all the way through — here is the ramp, here is the budget, here is the shortfall, here is the min_replicas that closes it — turns a caution into a procedure.
  2. Per-phase timings across model sizes. The phase names are published and the totals are published, but not the split. Knowing whether a 10-minute frontier cold start is mostly download or mostly warmup tells you which lever to pull.
  3. A section on what to do when the answer is "you can't." The post is honest that the autoscaler cannot win this one. It is a short step from there to the four levers, and that is the part a developer takes to their own architecture review.
